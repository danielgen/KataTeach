Abstract
Machine-learning systems trained to play Go can reach superhuman performance and produce skilful moves whose rationale is difficult to express in human terms. This creates a central problem for interpretability: a human concept may be predictable from a neural activation without being functionally involved in producing the model’s behaviour.
This project tests that distinction in KataGo. Three variables operationalising *tenuki*, forcing, and urgency were recomputed under fixed contracts from raw game records. Logistic-regression probes were trained on the final 512-channel trunk representation using 50,503 positions from 500 policy-play games. Five outer game-grouped folds, four inner game-grouped folds, and fold-local preprocessing were used to estimate out-of-game performance and select regularisation. A separate cohort of 150 games was generated only after the causal protocol was frozen; 50 games were used to calibrate controls and 100 untouched games were used for the final intervention test.
All three operational labels were strongly linearly predictable from the final trunk activations. Combined global-and-selected-location probes achieved mean ROC-AUCs of 0.958 for the distance-based tenuki label, 0.964 for the opponent-reply concentration forcing proxy, and 0.971 for the regional policy concentration urgency proxy. The selected-location vector outperformed global pooling for tenuki and forcing, whereas urgency was almost completely recoverable from globally pooled activations.
The predeclared causal hypothesis did not pass. For the tenuki-distance proxy, positive intervention doses were expected to increase legal-board policy mass on moves at least six Manhattan intersections from the preceding move. Instead, the label-balanced all-dose slope was negative (−0.000872 policy-mass units per dose, 95% game-bootstrap CI [−0.001028, −0.000719]). At every non-zero dose, every evaluated position changed in the sign opposite to the prediction. The trained direction also failed the predeclared comparisons with 100 random channel directions (one-sided empirical *p* = 0.703) and 50 shuffled spatial masks (*p* = 1.000).
The corrected evidence therefore supports linear accessibility of the three operational variables but not concept-specific causal steering by the tested tenuki direction. The result strengthens the project’s central methodological conclusion: decodability is insufficient evidence that a model uses the decoded direction when producing behaviour. Interpretability is consequently treated as constrained mediation rather than recovery of KataGo’s authentic “thoughts”; when the causal constraint fails, the stronger explanatory claim must be withheld.
1. Introduction: Alien Competence and the Problem of Explanation
The success of neural systems in the game of Go marked not only a change in competitive performance, but also a shift in epistemic authority. AlphaGo’s defeat of Lee Sedol in 2016 demonstrated that a system combining deep neural networks with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) could outperform one of the strongest human players in a domain long considered resistant to brute-force computation (Silver et al., 2016). 
Yet the significance of this achievement extended beyond winning. AlphaGo and its successors regularly selected moves that initially appeared inefficient, premature, or contrary to established Go knowledge, but were later recognised as strategically effective. In such cases, the practical success of the move preceded its human intelligibility.

Move 37 in the second game between AlphaGo and Lee Sedol became a prominent example: expert commentators initially found the move difficult to interpret, yet subsequent play supported its strategic value. It is better understood as an emblem of a broader AI-influenced change in Go rather than as that change’s sole cause. In subsequent years, professional and amateur players increasingly studied AI-recommended patterns, including locally balanced *joseki* involving early 3–3 invasions. These historical claims require scholarly sources in the final bibliography; the server-frequency image in the Appendix is illustrative rather than general evidence.

Neural Go systems have expanded the space of credible play, but they have not necessarily made that space understandable. Human players can imitate AI-recommended openings, invasions, or positional choices without understanding why they work or under which conditions they should be repeated. The result is a distinctive form of epistemic dependence: the system can function as an authoritative source of judgement even where its apparent reasoning cannot be reconstructed in familiar strategic terms.
This problem is not solved merely by attaching a plausible description to an AI move. Go players already possess a rich vocabulary through which moves can be interpreted: a move may build influence, preserve sente, answer an urgent local threat, create a forcing sequence, prepare a sacrifice, or tenuki by leaving one area to play elsewhere. Such concepts can illuminate relations among stones, regions, and future possibilities. However, their successful application by a human observer does not show that the neural network represents or uses the same concepts. A sufficiently flexible commentator may be able to explain almost any strong move after the fact. The explanation may therefore reveal more about the interpreter’s conceptual scheme than about the computation responsible for the move.
This is the danger of explanation as projection. Post-hoc explanations can be persuasive while remaining disconnected from the internal processes that generate a model’s output (Rudin, 2019). The risk is especially pronounced for neural networks, whose behaviour emerges from high-dimensional, distributed representations rather than from explicit symbolic rules. Even where a recognisable concept can be predicted from neural activations, the network may not rely on the corresponding information when producing its policy. Linear accessibility is therefore not equivalent to functional importance.
This dissertation investigates the following question:
To what extent do human-interpretable Go concepts correspond to representations within KataGo’s neural network, and does intervening on those representations causally alter concept-relevant policy behaviour?
The project distinguishes three progressively stronger forms of evidence. First, heuristic correspondence asks whether observable board and model outputs can be used to operationalise a human Go concept. Second, representational decodability asks whether that concept can be predicted from KataGo’s internal activations using a linear probe. Third, causal relevance asks whether intervention along the learned direction changes KataGo’s policy in a manner predicted by the concept. This distinction is important because a probe may recover information that is present in an activation without identifying a feature that the network functionally uses.
The project makes four principal contributions:
It develops a scalable, weakly supervised pipeline linking operational definitions of Go concepts to KataGo’s spatial neural activations. 
It empirically distinguishes linear decodability from causal influence rather than treating probe performance as sufficient evidence of representation. 
It evaluates a learned tenuki-associated direction in a frozen, held-out causal protocol and shows that the direction fails the predicted-sign and control-specificity criteria, despite strong probe performance. 
Finally, it develops a philosophical interpretation of concept-based explainability as constrained mediation: a disciplined construction of relations among human concepts, neural activations, and observable behaviour, rather than transparent access to the model’s authentic “thoughts”.
The scope of these claims must be carefully limited. The experiments examine KataGo’s neural-network component, primarily through single forward evaluations, rather than the complete search-enhanced playing system. Although this neural policy is extremely capable by ordinary human standards, the experimental model should not be straightforwardly equated with the full superhuman KataGo agent described in competitive settings. 
Full KataGo play results from repeated neural evaluations embedded within MCTS. The present study instead examines the policy produced by a single neural evaluation, allowing interventions to be localised to the network while excluding any corrective or amplifying effect of search.
Moreover, while the project originated in questions about trust, recognition, and human–AI understanding, it does not empirically test whether its explanations improve human trust, learning, or decision-making. A proposed player questionnaire was removed from the final scope so that the study could first address the logically prior question of representational and causal grounding.
The following chapter situates the project within research on Go AI, explainability, concept-based interpretation, distributed representation, and relational accounts of understanding. The dissertation then describes the project’s methodological development, the construction of weakly supervised concept labels, the training of linear probes, and the design of a held-out causal intervention. The results contrast strong decodability with a failed confirmatory steering hypothesis. The final discussion considers what this dissociation establishes, what it leaves unresolved, and how causally grounded explanations might later be evaluated through human-centred research.
2. Literature and Conceptual Framework
2.1 Go AI and the epistemic gap
AlphaGo’s defeat of Lee Sedol demonstrated that deep neural networks combined with tree search could achieve superhuman performance in a domain previously regarded as dependent on human intuition and accumulated strategic knowledge (Silver et al., 2016). Its significance was therefore not limited to competitive success. AlphaGo altered the distribution of epistemic authority within Go: professional players increasingly encountered moves whose effectiveness could be demonstrated through play or evaluation before their strategic meaning could be articulated. Subsequent systems did not merely automate established knowledge but contributed to changing what counted as credible Go strategy.
KataGo extends this development while offering unusual opportunities for interpretability research. As an open-source Go engine, it exposes not only a policy over possible moves but also value estimates, ownership predictions, and internal neural activations (LightVector, 2021). These outputs provide evidence about how the model evaluates a position, but they do not automatically translate into human concepts such as thickness, shape, influence, urgency, or sacrifice. The model is optimised to predict and select strong moves, not to organise its computation according to the vocabulary through which human players teach and discuss the game.
This mismatch is complicated by the fact that human Go understanding is itself plural. Garrett (2023) argues that a Go position need not possess one complete and uniquely correct explanation: different players may attend to territory, influence, tactical sequences, weaknesses, or future development while still understanding the position competently. Zhou et al. (2023), meanwhile, demonstrate the possibility of extracting information from a neural Go system to support human learning, but also reveal the additional work required to transform model behaviour into pedagogically meaningful representations. Go is therefore valuable as an interpretability domain not because it eliminates interpretation, but because it makes the gap between successful behaviour and human conceptual understanding unusually visible.
2.2 Explainability, faithfulness, and persuasive plausibility
Explainable AI encompasses methods serving substantially different purposes. Some explanations aim primarily to make a model’s output comprehensible to a user; others attempt to describe information represented within the model; still others seek to identify mechanisms that causally contribute to the output. These aims may overlap, but they should not be treated as equivalent.
Post-hoc attribution methods such as LIME and SHAP provide a useful illustration. LIME approximates a model locally using an interpretable surrogate, while SHAP assigns importance scores to input features using a game-theoretic framework. Such methods can identify relationships between inputs and predictions without exposing the complete computation that generated the prediction. Their usefulness therefore depends on the question being asked. An attribution may help a user recognise which parts of an input were influential while remaining an approximation constructed around the behaviour of a more complex system.
Rudin (2019) warns that post-hoc explanations of black-box models may be mistaken for faithful descriptions of their operation. A convincing explanation can produce confidence even when the explanatory artefact is incomplete, unstable, or detached from the model’s actual decision process. This concern is particularly relevant when explanations are expressed in natural language or familiar domain concepts. Human readers may accept an explanation because it resembles the reasoning of an expert, not because it accurately tracks the system’s internal computation.
Vainio-Pekka et al. (2023) situate such questions within a broader AI ethics context in which explainability is connected to values including accountability, autonomy, trust, and the capacity to contest automated decisions. From this perspective, explanation is not a purely technical property. It mediates how users understand a system’s authority and decide whether to defer to it. An explanation that is persuasive but unfaithful may therefore be ethically worse than opacity, because it can create an unwarranted impression of understanding.
Three standards should consequently be distinguished. First, an explanation may be intelligible, in the sense that users find it comprehensible or useful. Second, it may be representationally grounded, meaning that the relevant distinction can be recovered from internal model states. Third, it may identify causally operative structure, such that manipulating the proposed representation predictably changes the model’s behaviour. This dissertation concentrates on the latter two standards. Intelligibility remains important, but without representational and causal constraints it may measure persuasive plausibility more than explanatory faithfulness.
2.3 Concept-based interpretability
Concept-based interpretability attempts to bridge neural computation and human understanding by introducing an intermediate vocabulary of meaningful properties. Rather than explaining a prediction through individual pixels, neurons, or activation values, concept-based methods ask whether a model’s behaviour can be related to categories that domain experts already use.
Concept Bottleneck Models provide one influential example. In their standard form, CBMs predict a set of human-labelled concepts before using those concepts to predict the final target (Koh et al., 2020). This architecture creates an inspectable intermediate layer and permits targeted interventions: a user can correct a predicted concept and observe whether the final output changes. In principle, this offers a more transparent relation between evidence, concepts, and decisions than an unconstrained latent representation.
Related work has applied domain concepts to game explanation. Kim et al. (2024), for example, use concept-guided methods to generate and evaluate chess commentary, seeking to narrow the gap between expert analysis and language-model explanations. Earlier concept-activation approaches, including Testing with Concept Activation Vectors, similarly investigate whether activation-space directions corresponding to user-defined concepts influence a model’s predictions. These approaches motivated the present project’s initial interest in learning directions associated with Go concepts and using them as explanatory intermediaries.
The promise of concept methods is clear. They offer a vocabulary at an appropriate human level of abstraction, permit comparison across examples, and can support targeted inspection or intervention. Nevertheless, the insertion or discovery of a named concept does not establish that it faithfully captures the model’s computation. A predefined concept set may omit information needed for the task. Concepts may overlap, depend on context, or fail to correspond to separable neural features. A model may also bypass an interpretable bottleneck through residual or latent channels, preserving predictive accuracy without relying on the named concepts in the intended manner.
There is therefore a risk of semantic imposition. Researchers may attach a human label to a statistical direction because it correlates with labelled examples, even though the direction also contains unrelated information or reflects a simpler confound. In Go, a probe for tenuki, for instance, might detect distance from the previous move without representing the strategic judgement that a local position can safely be left. Concept-based explanations should consequently be understood as hypotheses about correspondence, not as immediate evidence that the model “uses” the same concepts as human players.
2.4 Distributed representation, superposition, and world models
The expectation that meaningful concepts should correspond to individual neurons or clean symbolic units is difficult to reconcile with contemporary accounts of neural representation. Elhage et al. (2022) use toy models to show how networks can encode more features than they possess dimensions by representing features in superposition. Under this account, individual activation dimensions need not carry stable, independent meanings. A concept may instead be distributed across multiple dimensions, while a single dimension may participate in representing several partially overlapping features.
This matters for concept-based interpretation because the absence of a dedicated “tenuki neuron” or “urgency channel” does not imply that the relevant structure is absent. Conversely, finding a neuron or linear direction correlated with a concept does not establish that the representation is exclusive, complete, or causally necessary. Neural representations may support many possible linear readouts, some corresponding to information that is merely preserved rather than actively used downstream.
Research on emergent world representations provides evidence that predictive systems can nonetheless develop structured internal states. Li et al. (2022) showed that a sequence model trained to predict moves in the game Othello developed internal representations corresponding to board state, despite receiving no explicit symbolic board representation. Moreover, interventions on these representations could alter subsequent predictions in ways consistent with counterfactual board states. This result is significant because it moves beyond identifying decodable information: intervention provides evidence that the discovered representation is functionally connected to model behaviour.
The distinction between probing and intervention is central to the present study. A linear probe tests whether a variable can be recovered from activations. It does not by itself show that the model relies on the probe direction, because representations can contain incidental, redundant, or correlated information. Intervention raises the evidential standard by asking whether modifying a direction produces a predicted behavioural effect. Even then, the interpretation requires controls, since sufficiently large perturbations may alter outputs through generic disruption rather than concept-specific influence.
The relevant question is therefore not whether a discrete human concept is literally stored inside the network, but whether a stable representational direction corresponds to it strongly enough to support prediction and controlled intervention. Such a direction remains an approximation to a distributed process, but it may nevertheless constitute a meaningful point of contact between neural computation and human interpretation.
2.5 Relational understanding: mediation rather than revelation
The remaining question is how such points of contact should be understood philosophically. A common metaphor treats interpretability as revelation: the model possesses an internal reason, and the purpose of explanation is to uncover and translate it. This metaphor presupposes that reasons exist as determinate objects inside the network and that a sufficiently successful method can recover them without substantially transforming them.
A relational framing offers a different account. Rovelli (2023) argues, in the context of quantum theory, that properties should not always be understood as intrinsic attributes possessed independently of interaction. Rather, properties emerge through relations between systems. Applied cautiously to AI interpretability, this suggests that meaning need not exist either entirely inside the model or entirely in the human observer. An explanation can instead arise through a relation among the model’s activations, observable features of the environment, an investigator’s conceptual vocabulary, and the behavioural consequences of intervention.
This perspective also accommodates Garrett’s (2023) pluralism about Go explanation. If different players can understand the same move through distinct but productive conceptual frameworks, then explanation need not reproduce one canonical internal narrative. Nevertheless, pluralism does not mean that every narrative is equally valid. Explanations can be constrained by the position, by the model’s representations, by predictive performance, and by the effects of controlled manipulation.
The reflective work preceding this project developed a similar concern with recognition, process, and relational intelligence. Trusting the Process considered the need to combine technical and philosophical methods when investigating forms of intelligence that do not naturally fit inherited categories. On the Ethics of Intelligence examined recognition as an ethical act through which the boundaries of intelligence are negotiated. AI Knows Its Own Voice explored how model behaviour may exhibit structured forms of self-relation without licensing straightforward anthropomorphic conclusions. These reflections motivate the present framework, but they do not function as independent empirical evidence. Their role is to articulate why interpretability should avoid both reductionism and projection.
Accordingly, this dissertation treats explanation as mediation rather than revelation. Revelation would claim to recover KataGo’s authentic reasons in a human-readable form. Mediation instead constructs a disciplined relation among human Go concepts, operational labels, neural activations, and policy behaviour. The relation is constrained when a concept can be decoded, when intervention produces the predicted effect, and when alternative explanations such as random perturbation or spatial misalignment are weakened by controls. It does not establish semantic identity between a human concept and a neural representation.
This framework yields three empirical possibilities. Some human concepts may be meaningful descriptions of positions but not decodable from the chosen activations. Others may be decodable while lacking evidence of causal relevance. The strongest case requires not merely successful classification, but concept-specific behavioural change under appropriately designed interventions and controls. These possibilities determine the experimental structure adopted in the following chapters.

3. From Explanation Generation to Causal Concept Testing
The final methodology emerged from a series of exploratory approaches to translating KataGo’s behaviour into human-interpretable concepts. Rather than treating these attempts as independent experiments, this section presents them as stages in refining the project’s evidential standard. Each approach exposed a limitation in generating explanations from either human labels or learned representations, motivating the eventual focus on linear decodability and controlled causal intervention.
3.1 Visualisation and model instrumentation
The first stage involved developing the infrastructure required to inspect KataGo beyond its selected move. Policy-based games were generated and stored as SGF files alongside move-by-move board states. The pipeline extracted policy distributions, value estimates, ownership predictions, and spatial activations from the network’s final trunk layer. These outputs were visualised as board-aligned heatmaps, allowing neural and model-derived quantities to be compared with stones, regions, and candidate moves. This instrumentation did not itself constitute an explanation method, but enabled the subsequent experiments by making internal outputs accessible and spatially interpretable.
3.2 Human annotation
An initial approach sought to ground explanations directly in human Go knowledge. A browser-based annotation interface was developed through which positions and moves could be labelled using an ontology containing concepts such as influence, invasion, shape, profitability, connection, and tenuki. This process revealed several practical and conceptual difficulties. Annotation was slow, limiting the size of the resulting dataset, while many concepts depended on game phase, surrounding stones, and anticipated continuations rather than on locally observable features alone. Knowledgeable players could also reasonably disagree about whether a move exemplified a particular concept or which concept best characterised it.
Such disagreement was not simply annotation failure. It reflected the situated and plural character of Go understanding discussed by Garrett (2023). Nevertheless, even strong inter-annotator agreement would only establish consistency among human interpretations. It would not demonstrate that KataGo internally organised positions according to the same distinctions.
3.3 Concept Bottleneck Models
The project subsequently explored Concept Bottleneck Models, inspired by Koh et al. (2020), as a way of explicitly inserting human-readable concepts between KataGo’s activations and its outputs. Several variants were considered. A labelled bottleneck relied exclusively on predefined human concepts; a latent bottleneck learned unconstrained intermediate features; and a hybrid model combined human-labelled concepts with additional learned dimensions.
The strictly labelled bottleneck provided an interpretable architecture but inherited noise and ambiguity from the annotations. More importantly, the selected concept set could not adequately capture all information required to reproduce KataGo’s policy. Introducing latent dimensions improved predictive capacity, but weakened the explanatory guarantee: the model could route decision-relevant information through uninterpreted channels while making limited use of the named concepts. The hybrid model could therefore appear interpretable while bypassing its interpretable component. This exposed a distinction between architectural interpretability and explanatory faithfulness. Merely placing named variables inside a model does not demonstrate that they genuinely mediate its behaviour.
3.4 Unsupervised spatial concept discovery
A further approach attempted to avoid imposing a predetermined ontology. Spatial models were trained to learn latent concept maps while predicting KataGo’s policy or value outputs. Some learned channels appeared to capture coherent whole-board regularities, suggesting that structured spatial information could emerge without direct concept supervision. However, these patterns were not consistently interpretable. Some training runs produced inactive channels, while others were dominated by a small number of broadly responsive features. Learned dimensions did not reliably converge on ordinary Go concepts or admit stable human labels.
These results were compatible with accounts of distributed representation and superposition: strategically relevant information may be encoded across overlapping directions rather than separated into human-recognisable units. Unsupervised discovery therefore avoided semantic imposition but did not, by itself, solve the problem of explanation.
3.5 Refinement of scope and removal of the human questionnaire
The finalised project proposal included a within-subject questionnaire comparing AI moves presented alone with moves accompanied by concept-based explanations. The planned study would have measured perceived clarity, usefulness, connection to the system, trust calibration, and qualitative responses to the explanations.
Following supervisory discussion, this component was removed from the final scope. Building a functioning explanation layer, validating its relationship to internal representations, and testing whether it affected policy behaviour already constituted a substantial empirical project. More importantly, evaluating explanations with users before establishing their representational and causal grounding risked measuring persuasive plausibility rather than explanatory faithfulness. Participants might prefer an articulate explanation even if it had been imposed after the fact or was unrelated to the model’s computation.
The revised project therefore prioritised the logically prior question: whether the proposed concepts could be recovered from KataGo’s activations and whether manipulating the associated representations influenced behaviour in the predicted direction. The removal of the questionnaire was consequently not an abandonment of the original concern with trust and understanding, but a refinement of the research sequence. Human-centred evaluation remains important future work, but it should follow rather than substitute for evidence that the explanation layer is grounded in the system being explained.

4. Methods
This chapter summarises the experimental design; Appendix C provides the complete end-to-end documentation of the weakly supervised concept construction, the probing strategy, and the activation-manipulation experiment, with implementation and artifact references.
4.1 System and model setting
Experiments used the repository’s PyTorch KataGo implementation with `daniele_experiment/model.ckpt` (SHA-256 `9476214872d78c80b53605cf5a654004faa7d59b6a743fd5b68942c36dd4ace3`). The loader selected the checkpoint’s raw model weights rather than stochastic weight averaging. The embedded version-15 model configuration has 512 trunk channels and a Mish activation. Because KataGo ordinarily embeds neural evaluations within Monte Carlo tree search, the present study deliberately examined the direct neural policy without MCTS. Its claims therefore concern the network policy, not the complete search-enhanced KataGo agent.

Games were played on 19 × 19 boards. The saved `act_trunkfinal` tensor is the output of the final trunk activation and has shape 512 × 19 × 19 before the policy and value heads. The pipeline also stored legal-move policy outputs, ownership and value-related outputs, board state, and move metadata. Replaying one deterministic position from each of the 500 development games with the supplied checkpoint yielded a maximum absolute trunk-activation error of 3.50 × 10⁻⁵, below the prospectively fixed 10⁻⁴ tolerance. This is empirical compatibility evidence for the sampled saved activations; it does not prove that this checkpoint originally generated every historical activation.
4.2 Dataset generation and leakage prevention
The development corpus comprised 500 pre-existing policy-play games and 50,503 move records. Because these games had already been inspected during exploratory work, they were treated exclusively as development data. Each record includes a stable game and row identifier, the internal board location (`move_loc`), the corresponding row-major neural-policy index (`idx361`), model outputs, and the pre-move trunk activation. The distinction between `move_loc` and `idx361` matters because KataGo’s padded internal board coordinates are not interchangeable with the 0–360 index of a 19 × 19 tensor. All local features in the final analysis were rebuilt using `idx361`.

The causal protocol was frozen before generating a separate 150-game cohort. These games used direct policy sampling without MCTS: sampling was restricted to the top ten legal actions with raw policy probability at least 0.01, and temperature declined from 1.2 to 0.8 over the first 60 moves. Generation used 19 × 19 `GameState.RULES_TT`, unique prospectively enumerated random seeds, a 400-move cap, and the same hashed checkpoint. A frozen permutation assigned 50 games to control calibration and 100 to causal testing. There was no refill after labels or outputs were inspected.

Successive positions from one game are statistically dependent. Probe training therefore used game-grouped folds throughout. The fresh 150 games were excluded from feature scaling, hyperparameter selection, threshold selection, and direction fitting. The causal analysis then selected exactly one position per fresh game, so its game-clustered uncertainty calculation also operates at the independent sampling unit.
4.3 Weakly supervised concept construction
Manual annotation was too limited for large-scale probe training, so the project initially constructed more than twenty weak labels from board state and KataGo outputs. The validity-v5 primary analysis does not treat all of those historical fields as equivalently validated. It recomputes three variables from raw records under versioned contracts and keeps the remaining 24 migrated labels in a physically separate exploratory run.

The three canonical variables are:

- `tenuki_distance6`: a selected board move is positive when its Manhattan distance from the most recent non-pass move is at least six intersections. This is a geometric proxy for *tenuki*, not the complete strategic concept.

- `reply_peak95`: a selected move is positive when the opponent’s post-move policy, masked to legal replies plus pass and renormalised on that support, assigns strictly more than 0.95 to its most probable reply. This is a response-concentration proxy for forcing, not forcing itself.

- `regional_policy_peak`: the current player’s pre-move policy is masked and renormalised over legal board moves, summed within four corner, four side, and one centre region, and reduced to the largest regional mass. The upper 15% of eligible development values is labelled positive. This is a regional concentration proxy for urgency, not urgency itself.

Each variable is therefore tied to an explicit time point and probability support: tenuki uses board geometry, forcing uses the opponent’s policy after the observed move, and urgency uses the current player’s policy before it. All three are paired with the pre-move trunk representation because the probes ask whether the selected move’s operational label is predictable before that move is made. These definitions do not provide semantic ground truth; they create reproducible hypotheses relating human vocabulary to measurable variables.
4.4 Concept labels and representation extraction
Three pre-move representations were extracted for every eligible row. The global representation is the spatial mean of each of the 512 channels. The local representation is the 512-channel vector at the selected move’s corrected `idx361` coordinate. The combined representation concatenates the global and local blocks into 1,024 features. Rebuilding this parquet corrected an earlier implementation in which padded `move_loc` values had been used as if they were row-major tensor indices; all results produced before that correction are excluded from validity-v5 evidence.

Forcing used all 50,503 positions, of which 4,934 were positive. Tenuki excluded each game’s first row because it has no previous non-pass move; urgency excluded the same 500 rows because the historical game format has no persisted pre-move policy for the first recorded move. Both therefore used 50,003 positions. Tenuki had 17,450 positives and urgency had 7,502 positives. Counts, labels, features, fold assignments, model outputs, and predictions are hash-bound in the canonical run manifests.
4.5 Linear probes
Balanced logistic-regression probes were evaluated with nested grouped cross-validation. The outer loop comprised five folds grouped by game. Within each outer training partition, four further game-grouped folds selected `C` from 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10 by mean inner-fold average precision. The classification threshold maximising F1 was chosen from inner out-of-fold predictions. Standardisation parameters, regularisation, and thresholds were therefore learned without any position from the corresponding outer test games. There was no probability calibration and no performance gate: every canonical result, including any null result, was retained.

Performance is reported as the mean and sample standard deviation across the five outer folds. Confidence intervals use 2,000 game-cluster bootstrap draws, resampling games within outer folds and then averaging fold metrics. These intervals are conditional on the fitted out-of-fold predictions: the probes, scalers, hyperparameters, and thresholds are not refitted in each bootstrap draw. Paired bootstrap draws were used for global-only, corrected local-only, and combined ablations.

A post-run audit found a reporting-only defect in the frozen vectorised average-precision bootstrap: when a resample assigned zero weight to the highest score group, an irrelevant 0/0 precision value propagated to the AP sum. The frozen reporter and its original output were retained unchanged. A versioned erratum safely assigns zero contribution to empty groups, recomputes only AP bootstrap records with the original seeds and settings, and verifies byte-for-byte equality of every non-AP result field. Probe fitting, point estimates, ROC-AUC intervals, and the causal analysis were unaffected.

A probe coefficient is a statistical direction, not automatically a causal variable. High held-out performance shows that an operational label is linearly accessible from the chosen activation summary. It does not show that KataGo naturally relies on that direction, because the probe may exploit correlated, redundant, or downstream information. This distinction motivated the prospectively held-out intervention.
4.6 Activation intervention
Tenuki was the sole confirmatory causal test. A local probe and scaler were fitted using only the 500 development games. If the standardised probe score is $s(x)=\beta^{\mathsf T}((x-\mu)/\sigma)+b$, then its raw-activation gradient is $g=\beta/\sigma$. The intervention used $\delta=g/(g^{\mathsf T}g)$, so $g^{\mathsf T}\delta=1$: an unmasked unit dose changes the linear probe score by one unit. This conversion is required because the learned coefficients otherwise live in standardised feature space.

For each held-out position, a spatial mask was positive on legal board actions satisfying the exact distance-at-least-six contract and negative on the remaining legal board actions. Positive and negative parts had equal total mass, illegal locations were zero, and the active mask was root-mean-square normalised. The saved trunk tensor was modified as $h'_{c,y,x}=h_{c,y,x}+d\,m_{y,x}\delta_c$ at doses $d\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$, then passed through the unchanged policy head. The frozen primary readout was total policy mass on legal board actions satisfying the same distance contract. The predicted result was a positive all-dose slope: positive doses should increase far-region mass and negative doses should decrease it.

The saved-trunk policy backend was checked against six full-network replays before evaluation. The maximum activation error was zero and the maximum absolute policy error was 6.56 × 10⁻⁷, below the frozen 10⁻⁶ tolerance. Operational alignment checks passed for all 150 selected calibration and test positions.
4.7 Controls and robustness tests
Two control families separated the learned channel direction from the researcher-designed spatial contrast. Fifty controls retained the learned direction but independently shuffled the active mask values at each position. One hundred controls retained the aligned mask but replaced the learned channel direction with a deterministic random direction whose active block norm was matched.

Control dose multipliers were selected only on the 50 calibration games to approximate the trained intervention’s mean legal-plus-pass policy Jensen–Shannon divergence at each nominal dose. All 750 control-by-dose calibration combinations succeeded. The multipliers were then frozen and transferred to the 100 causal-test games; no exact disruption-matching claim is made for the test set.

The primary statistic was the ordinary-least-squares slope of paired readout change across all five doses, weighting the 50 label-negative and 50 label-positive test games equally. The predeclared headline required all three conditions: a strictly positive trained slope, a one-sided empirical random-direction *p* value no greater than 0.05, and a one-sided empirical shuffle *p* value no greater than 0.05. Game-stratified bootstrap uncertainty used 2,000 draws. No individual dose, secondary readout, or post-hoc sign reversal was permitted to replace this conjunction.
5. Results
Results are reported in two evidential tiers. The primary analysis comprises the three variables recomputed under canonical contracts and the prospectively held-out intervention for the tenuki-distance proxy. The separate 24-label historical analysis is reported only as exploratory evidence. Within the primary analysis, the results address three successive questions: whether each operational label was linearly predictable in held-out games; whether intervention along the tenuki-associated direction changed the matched policy readout in the predicted direction; and whether any such effect exceeded the two predeclared control distributions. The probe results were strong, but the confirmatory causal hypothesis was not supported.

5.2 Global, selected-location, and combined ablations

Paired ablations clarified how predictive information was distributed across the three extraction summaries. For tenuki, the selected-location vector exceeded global pooling in ROC-AUC by 0.0979 (95% paired bootstrap CI [0.0937, 0.1013]), while adding the global block to that vector produced a further gain of 0.0070 [0.0062, 0.0078]. For forcing, the corresponding differences were 0.0529 [0.0483, 0.0579] and 0.0066 [0.0057, 0.0075]. Tenuki and forcing were therefore more linearly predictable from the move-indexed vector than from global pooling, with a smaller complementary gain from combining both summaries. This comparison does not by itself anatomically localise the information, because a trunk vector at one board coordinate can have a broad receptive field.

Urgency showed the opposite pattern. The selected-location ROC-AUC was 0.0196 below the global result [−0.0212, −0.0179]. Adding the selected-location block to the global representation increased ROC-AUC by only 0.0010 [0.0004, 0.0017], while the corresponding AP difference was 0.0014 [−0.0020, 0.0048]. In this ablation, the urgency proxy was therefore almost completely recoverable from global pooling; the combined model added little practical discrimination.

The frozen positive-slope prediction was not observed in the prospectively held-out cohort. 5.4 Predeclared control comparisons and decision

Control dose multipliers were Jensen–Shannon-calibrated on the 50 calibration games and transferred to the test games without rematching. On the test set, the ratios of control to trained mean policy Jensen–Shannon divergence across non-zero dose combinations ranged from 0.687 to 2.293 for random directions and from 0.795 to 1.901 for shuffled masks. The controls remained within the same order of downstream disruption, but matching transferred only approximately to the test cohort.

For the 100 aligned-mask random channel directions, the mean slope was 0.000097 (SD 0.000924; range [−0.001213, 0.001263]). Seventy random slopes were at least as extreme as the trained slope in the predeclared positive direction, yielding one-sided empirical *p* = (1 + 70)/(1 + 100) = 0.703. Thirty random directions were more negative than the trained direction, so the data provided no evidence that the inverse effect was specific to the learned channel direction.

For the 50 learned-direction shuffled-mask controls, the mean slope was 0.0000005 (SD 0.0000484; range [−0.000154, 0.000105]). All 50 were at least as extreme as the trained slope in the predeclared positive direction, giving one-sided empirical *p* = 1.000. Every shuffled-mask slope had smaller absolute magnitude than the trained negative slope, but absolute magnitude and post-hoc sign reversal were not predeclared success criteria.

None of the three required conditions was therefore satisfied: the trained slope was not positive, and neither one-sided control comparison reached the predeclared threshold. The intervention for the tenuki-distance proxy did not pass the headline-support criterion.

6. Discussion
6.1 What the tenuki-distance-proxy result establishes
Tenuki produced a sharp dissociation. Its operational label was strongly predictable in unseen development games, especially from the corrected selected-location vector. Yet a direction fitted without any fresh game failed when subjected to a frozen single-evaluation policy prediction on 100 untouched games. The effect was monotonic and estimated precisely, but it was small, opposite-signed, and not more predicted-direction extreme than the random-direction controls.

This result is informative because the protocol could reject the intended interpretation. It shows that probe orientation in label space does not determine how a spatially broadcast intervention will affect downstream policy. The coefficient may be an effective separator because it tracks correlated or redundant information without forming a downstream control axis. Alternatively, the externally imposed positive-far/negative-near mask may interact with the policy head differently from naturally occurring activation variation. These possibilities are not distinguished by the present experiment.

The larger absolute inverse slope relative to the shuffled masks is an exploratory observation, not a substitute success. It raises the post-hoc possibility that spatial alignment contributed to the inverse perturbation, but the controls were not exactly rematched on the test set and 30 random aligned directions produced still more negative slopes. The data therefore do not support renaming the negative effect as evidence that KataGo uses a “negative tenuki direction”.
6.2 What the result does not establish
The distance-at-least-six label is not identical to the human concept of *tenuki*. Human judgement also depends on whether the local position is settled, whether a threat can safely be ignored, who retains initiative, and whether the elsewhere move is strategically justified. Strong decoding may therefore reflect locality, available space, game phase, or correlated regional opportunity.

The intervention does not identify KataGo’s natural mediator. It externally applies a statistical vector with a researcher-designed spatial contrast. Even a successful predicted-direction intervention would have shown that the direction is causally usable when imposed, not that ordinary decisions are naturally mediated by precisely that axis. Because the frozen directional and specificity tests failed, the present evidence is weaker still: it shows linear accessibility and small perturbation sensitivity, not concept-relevant causal use.

Nor does the study establish strategic improvement or human explanatory value. The policy changes were tiny and almost never changed the top move; move quality under intervention was not tested. No participant study assessed whether these representations improve human learning, understanding, or trust calibration.
6.3 Decodability versus causal relevance
The probe results answer a narrower question than the original headline. They show that three precisely defined labels are linearly accessible from `trunkfinal` in held-out games. They do not establish that the labels exhaust the associated human concepts or that the fitted coefficients are directions the policy head naturally uses.

This distinction is particularly important for `reply_peak95` and `regional_policy_peak`, both of which are constructed from KataGo policies but at different time points. `regional_policy_peak` is calculated from the current pre-move policy immediately downstream of the saved trunk, so its high global decodability is architecturally unsurprising. By contrast, `reply_peak95` is calculated from a separate opponent-policy evaluation after the selected move; its pre-move decodability predicts a next-state response rather than recovering an output from the same head pass. The board-geometric tenuki label is not derived from a model output and therefore offered the cleanest causal hypothesis of the three. Its failure shows why even that cleaner case required intervention rather than semantic inference from AUC.

The correct evidential map is consequently asymmetric. Forcing and urgency have strong decodability evidence but no validity-v5 causal test. Tenuki has strong decodability evidence and a completed causal test that did not support the proposed direction. The historical labels add exploratory breadth but cannot repair this causal gap. This also clarifies the earlier Concept Bottleneck Model difficulty: attaching names to intermediate variables does not show that those variables faithfully mediate an output.
6.4 Explanation as constrained mediation
The failed test sharpens the account of explanation as constrained mediation. The project constructs a chain from a human concept to an operational variable, from that variable to a predictive activation direction, and from that direction to a predeclared behavioural consequence. A genuine constraint must be capable of breaking the chain. Here the first predictive link was strong, but the behavioural link failed in sign and specificity; the stronger causal explanation must therefore be withheld.

This avoids both projection and revelation. A projective account could redescribe the inverse effect after the fact and retain a persuasive tenuki story. A revelatory account could equate the high probe score with discovery of KataGo’s internal reason. Constrained mediation permits neither move: human categories can organise an empirical investigation, but their connection to model behaviour remains conditional on tests fixed independently of the result.

The philosophical contribution is therefore methodological rather than a claim to have translated KataGo’s thoughts. The study demonstrates how a concept explanation can advance from operationalisation to decodability and then fail at causal validation without the earlier evidence being erased. Whether a future, better-grounded relation supports human recognition, learning, or calibrated trust remains a separate human-centred question.
6.5 Limitations
Construct validity is the first limitation. Distance, opponent-reply concentration, and regional policy concentration capture only selected aspects of *tenuki*, forcing, and urgency. Both concentration labels are model-output-derived, but only the urgency proxy comes from the policy produced by the same pre-move trunk evaluation; the forcing proxy comes from a separate post-move opponent evaluation. The 24 additional historical labels have a stricter limitation: their legacy source fields were not rebuilt under the canonical contracts, so their results remain exploratory regardless of probe performance.

Internal validity is stronger for the frozen tenuki test but still bounded. The direction was fitted on 500 development games and tested on untouched games, yet the manipulation combined that learned direction with a researcher-designed contrastive mask. It tests that specified intervention, not natural mediation. Controls were matched to downstream policy disruption on 50 calibration games; their multipliers were transferred rather than rematched on the causal-test set. The causal sample contained only 100 positions, deliberately one per game and balanced by observed label, so its estimand is not a prevalence-weighted corpus average. Bootstrap intervals condition on fitted predictions or intervention outputs and do not repeat the full model-selection process.

Historical provenance is incomplete. The supplied checkpoint reproduced one sampled activation from every development game within the frozen tolerance, but this establishes empirical compatibility rather than original generator identity. Complete historical random seeds and generation provenance were unavailable. The fresh 150-game cohort has prospectively fixed seeds and checkpoint bindings, which is why only it supports the causal claim.

External validity is limited to one checkpoint, one late layer, a particular spatial mask and dose schedule, policy-play positions, and direct neural evaluation without MCTS. Results may differ across earlier layers, architectures, stronger or differently normalised interventions, human games, or search-enhanced play. Finally, human validity was not tested: no evidence here shows that probe or intervention information improves a player’s understanding, learning, or calibrated trust.
6.6 Human evaluation as future work
The questionnaire removed from the final scope could form the basis of a stronger future experiment. Rather than comparing only an AI move with and without an explanation, a study should include three conditions: the move alone; the move accompanied by a causally grounded concept explanation; and the move accompanied by an equally fluent but degraded or weakly grounded explanation.
This design would allow persuasive quality to be separated from representational grounding. Subjective measures could include perceived clarity, usefulness, trust, connection, and willingness to rely on the system. These should be combined with objective measures of understanding, including participants’ ability to predict the AI’s next move, transfer the explanation to related positions, identify misleading commentary, and calibrate trust when the model is wrong. Qualitative interviews could examine how players incorporate the explanation into their own Go reasoning.
The central future question is therefore not simply whether users prefer concept-based explanations, but whether causal grounding produces more accurate and transferable understanding than persuasive fluency alone.
7. Conclusion
This dissertation asked whether operationalisations of human Go concepts are accessible from KataGo’s final trunk representation and whether intervention along an associated direction changes policy in the predicted concept-relevant way. The answer differs across those two levels.

Three canonical labels operationalising *tenuki*, forcing, and urgency were strongly linearly predictable under nested game-grouped validation. This supports linear accessibility of those variables, not semantic identity with the human concepts. Twenty-four additional historical variables produced exploratory probe results but remain outside the primary claim because their migrated source fields have not received the same validation.

The sole prospective causal test evaluated the intervention for the tenuki-distance proxy on 100 untouched games. It failed: the all-dose slope was small and negative rather than positive, every non-zero-dose position moved opposite the predicted sign, and neither one-sided control comparison passed. The evidence therefore does not support concept-specific causal steering by the tested tenuki-associated direction. Forcing and urgency were not causally evaluated in validity v5.

The project’s central contribution is the evidential separation itself. A reproducible label can be highly decodable while its associated probe direction fails a frozen causal prediction. Treating explanation as constrained mediation requires accepting that failure rather than converting it into a post-hoc success. Future work can test better operationalisations, layers, mediation methods, models, and search settings before asking whether any resulting explanation improves human understanding or trust calibration.

Appendix C. End-to-end experimental documentation

This appendix documents the complete experimental pipeline in one place: the weakly supervised (Snorkel-style) concept construction, the linear-probing strategy, and the activation-manipulation strategy and its experiment design. It consolidates and supersedes the working documents kept alongside the code (`SNORKEL_FEATURES_DOCUMENTATION.md`, `LINEAR_PROBES_WORKFLOW.md`, `ACTIVATION_MANIPULATION.md`) and cross-references the standalone technical record `VALIDITY_V5_EXPERIMENTS_AND_RESULTS.md`, which lists the authoritative artifact paths for every number quoted in Chapter 5. Repository-relative implementation files are named so that each stage can be audited directly.

C.1 Pipeline overview

The pipeline has six stages, executed in this order:

1. **Game generation and instrumentation.** Policy-play games are generated without MCTS and every move record stores the board state, move metadata, legal-move policy, ownership and value outputs, and the pre-move `trunkfinal` activation (512 × 19 × 19). Implementation: `generate_games_dataset.py`, `common_utils.py`, `python/model_pytorch.py`.
2. **Weak concept labelling.** Snorkel-style labelling functions compute board-geometric and model-output-derived fields for every move; a declarative configuration turns selected fields into binary training labels. Implementation: `snorkel_board_positions.py`, `concepts.yaml`.
3. **Canonical label recomputation.** Three variables are rebuilt from raw records under versioned operational contracts, independently of the historical Snorkel output. Implementation: `operational_definitions.py`, `build_validated_labels.py`, `concepts_validated_v5.yaml`.
4. **Probe training and evaluation.** Balanced logistic-regression probes are trained on three activation summaries under nested game-grouped cross-validation. Implementation: `validated_probe_pipeline.py`.
5. **Direction construction and activation intervention.** The tenuki local-probe coefficients are converted into a raw-activation steering direction, combined with a contract-aligned spatial mask, and applied to held-out positions at fixed doses. Implementation: `validated_causal_eval.py`.
6. **Controls and confirmatory decision.** Two predeclared control families and a frozen three-part decision rule determine whether the intervention supports concept-specific steering. Implementation: `causal_controls.py`, `validated_causal_results_report.py`.

Stages 1–2 predate the validity-v5 refactor and produced the development corpus (500 games, 50,503 move records). Stages 3–6 constitute validity v5: the protocol in `artifacts/protocols/validity_v5.json` was frozen before a fresh cohort of 150 games was generated and split once into 50 control-calibration and 100 causal-test games.

C.2 Snorkel concept construction

C.2.1 Feature machinery

The labelling module computes 58 feature fields per move from four inputs: the board state before and after the move, KataGo’s ownership map, and KataGo’s policy. Three shared conventions underlie all fields:

- **Ownership normalisation.** Raw ownership is expressed from the current player’s perspective; every feature first renormalises it to the analysing player’s perspective so that positive values always mean “owned by the mover”.
- **Soft group enumeration.** Stones are grouped by physical 4-adjacency, then extended through empty points whose ownership exceeds 0.2, so that strategically connected stones form one unit. Each group carries a strength (mean ownership over its stones), a connectivity (mean ownership of nearby empty points), and an influence area and strength (points reachable through ownership paths). Groups before and after a move are matched by intersection-over-union. Tactical fields that require exact life-and-death semantics (`cut`, `atari`, liberties) instead use the deterministic `Board` connectivity.
- **Nine board regions.** The board is partitioned into four 6 × 6 corners, four sides, and a centre; regional fields aggregate territory and policy quantities within this partition.

Thresholds are fixed constants (for example, weak ownership 0.10, solid ownership 0.70, minimum ownership delta 0.05, “only move” policy margin 0.95) and are documented field-by-field in `SNORKEL_FEATURES_DOCUMENTATION.md`. The fields fall into seven families: territory (building, solidification, reduction, invasion), group dynamics (strength, connectivity, influence deltas), local tactics (cut, connect, extend, atari, liberties), attack (intensity, killing attacks, aji reduction), sacrifice (direct and indirect), policy-derived fields (regional urgency, forcing, tenuki), and regional breakdowns.

C.2.2 From feature fields to training labels

The configuration file `concepts.yaml` declaratively converts fields into binary labels using three rule types: **binary** (the field is already Boolean), **threshold** (the field exceeds a fixed value), and **quantile** (the top or bottom 15% of eligible values is positive). Quantile concepts may add row filters (for example, requiring an attacking move before ranking attack intensity), may stratify thresholds by game phase, and may either drop or keep middle-quantile rows as negatives. Each concept also declares a `feature_mode`: `pre` concepts are paired with the pre-move activation and describe an intention or situation, whereas `post` concepts are paired with the post-move activation and describe a move’s effect. This distinction later became an eligibility rule for causal testing (Section C.4.1).

C.2.3 Canonical recomputation versus historical migration

The validity-v5 refactor split this inventory into two evidential classes. Three variables were recomputed from raw game records under versioned, hash-bound contracts in `operational_definitions.py`, deliberately without reading any historical Snorkel output:

| Canonical contract | Definition | Relation to the legacy Snorkel field |
|---|---|---|
| `tenuki_distance6@2` | The recorded move is at least six Manhattan intersections from the most recent non-pass move. | Simplification: the legacy `tenuki` also required a region change and a higher-policy local follow-up near the previous move; the contract keeps only the auditable geometric predicate. |
| `reply_peak95@2` | After the recorded move, the opponent’s reply policy, masked to legal replies plus pass and renormalised, assigns strictly more than 0.95 to its top reply. | Same intent as the legacy `forcing` (computed on the post-move policy), with the probability support made explicit. |
| `regional_policy_peak@2` | The current player’s pre-move policy is renormalised over legal board moves, summed within the nine regions, and reduced to the maximum regional mass; the top 15% of eligible values is positive, with quantile cut-offs fitted only on training games at each cross-validation level. | Formalisation of the legacy regional `urgency` field with a fixed labelling rule. |

The remaining 24 configured labels (from 22 hash-checked migrated source fields) were retained only in a physically separate exploratory run: corrected features and the same probe machinery were applied, but the underlying Snorkel fields were not independently rebuilt or construct-validated, so those results are hypothesis-generating only (Section 5.5 and Appendix A). Twenty-two of the 24 use post-move activations, which can materially inflate separability for deterministic move-effect labels.

C.3 Probing strategy

C.3.1 Representations

Each eligible move contributes three summaries of its pre-move `trunkfinal` tensor: the **global** representation (spatial mean of each of the 512 channels), the **selected-location** representation (the 512-channel vector at the recorded move’s row-major coordinate `idx361`), and the **combined** representation (both blocks concatenated, 1,024 features). KataGo’s padded internal `move_loc` coordinates are not row-major tensor indices; an earlier implementation that used them as such was invalidated, and all validity-v5 features index the tensor as `trunkfinal[:, idx361 // 19, idx361 % 19]` after conversion and validation.

C.3.2 Training and evaluation protocol

Probes are class-balanced logistic regressions evaluated with nested cross-validation grouped by game, because successive positions within a game are statistically dependent. Five outer folds estimate out-of-game performance. Within each outer training partition, four inner game-grouped folds select the regularisation strength from {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10} by mean inner-fold average precision, and the classification threshold maximises F1 on inner out-of-fold predictions. Standardisation, hyperparameters, and thresholds are therefore learned without any position from the corresponding outer test games. There is no probability calibration and no performance gate: every result, including any null, is reported. Metrics are means over the five outer folds; 95% intervals use 2,000 game-cluster bootstrap draws conditional on the fitted out-of-fold predictions, with paired draws for representation ablations. A post-freeze erratum (`validated_results_report_apfix_v2`) corrected an average-precision bootstrap edge case without touching probes, predictions, or ROC-AUC results.

C.3.3 What a probe result can and cannot show

A probe coefficient is a statistical direction. High held-out performance establishes that an operational label is linearly accessible from the tested activation summary; it does not establish that the network uses that direction when producing behaviour, because the probe may exploit correlated, redundant, or downstream information. The ablation comparisons (global versus selected-location versus combined) describe how the accessible information is distributed across engineered summaries; they do not anatomically localise it, since a trunk vector at one coordinate can have a broad receptive field. These limits are exactly what motivated the causal stage.

C.4 Activation-manipulation strategy and experiment design

C.4.1 Exploratory phase and its lessons

Two exploratory tools preceded the confirmatory design and are quarantined from inferential use. `activation_manipulation.py` steered only the global mean-pool block of a probe direction, with dose measured in probe decision-score units, and played full 1-visit games at several doses; it established the readout vocabulary (policy Jensen–Shannon and L1 change, top-move flips, dose-response slopes) and a key eligibility rule: only `feature_mode: pre` probes may be steered, because the hook runs on the forward pass that chooses the next move, and steering a post-move effect concept would make a null result uninterpretable. `position_causal_eval.py` added position-bank evaluation stratified by label and probe score, spatially masked interventions (far-only and far-versus-near contrast masks), and three control ideas later formalised: shuffled masks, norm-matched random channel directions, and other-concept directions. Both tools evaluated in-sample positions with pre-`idx361` features and no frozen hypothesis, which is why their outputs were archived rather than reported.

C.4.2 Confirmatory design: hypothesis and freeze discipline

The validity-v5 protocol froze, before any fresh game existed: the single confirmatory target (the tenuki-distance proxy), the direction construction, the mask, the doses, the readout, the primary statistic, the control families, the calibration procedure, and a three-part decision rule. Headline support required all of: (1) a strictly positive trained-direction slope across all doses; (2) a one-sided random-direction empirical p-value at most 0.05; and (3) a one-sided shuffled-mask empirical p-value at most 0.05. No individual dose, secondary readout, or post-hoc sign reversal could substitute for this conjunction. The 150 fresh games were then generated with fixed seeds and checkpoint bindings and split once: 50 games for control calibration, 100 for the causal test, with no refill after inspection. One eligible position per game was selected deterministically, stratified by observed label (25/25 calibration, 50/50 test), so the estimand is an equal-label-weighted mean rather than a corpus average.

C.4.3 Direction, mask, dose, and readout

The local tenuki probe and its scaler were fitted on the 500 development games only. With standardised probe score $s(x)=\beta^{\mathsf T}((x-\mu)/\sigma)+b$, the raw-activation gradient is $g=\beta/\sigma$ and the intervention direction is $\delta=g/(g^{\mathsf T}g)$, so an unmasked unit dose changes the raw linear probe score by exactly one unit. For each position, a spatial mask was positive on legal board moves satisfying the distance-at-least-six contract and negative on the remaining legal board moves, with equal signed totals, zero weight on illegal points and pass, and RMS normalisation over the active support. The saved trunk tensor was perturbed as $h'_{c,y,x}=h_{c,y,x}+d\,m_{y,x}\delta_c$ at doses $d\in\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\}$ and passed through the unchanged policy head. The primary readout was total policy mass on legal board moves satisfying the same distance predicate, renormalised over legal board moves; the primary statistic was the ordinary-least-squares slope of paired readout change on dose, weighting the two label strata equally, with 2,000 stratified game-bootstrap draws for uncertainty. The tested object is therefore a composite of a learned channel direction and a researcher-designed spatial contrast, and the design assumes the selected-action direction generalises to unselected candidate locations — an extrapolation that was not independently tested.

C.4.4 Controls and integrity gates

Two control families isolate the two components of the composite intervention: 50 controls keep the learned direction but shuffle the mask within the legal support (does the spatial arrangement matter?), and 100 controls keep the aligned mask but substitute a norm-matched deterministic random channel direction (does the learned direction matter?). There is no random-direction-plus-shuffled-mask arm, so channel–mask interactions are not fully decomposed. Control dose multipliers were calibrated only on the 50 calibration games to approximate the trained intervention’s mean legal-plus-pass policy Jensen–Shannon divergence at each dose, then frozen and transferred to the test games; matching therefore held only approximately on the test cohort. Before evaluation, three integrity gates were passed: all 150 selected activations were bound to the rebuilt run provenance; label, mask, and readout alignment was verified on all 150 positions; and the saved-trunk policy backend reproduced six full-network replays with zero activation error and maximum policy error 6.56 × 10⁻⁷, below the frozen 10⁻⁶ tolerance.

C.5 Outcome and evidential boundary

The probe stage found all three canonical labels highly linearly predictable (Section 5.1); the confirmatory intervention failed all three predeclared conditions, with a small negative slope, unanimous opposite-sign position-level changes at every non-zero dose, and control p-values of 0.703 and 1.000 (Sections 5.3–5.4). The end-to-end structure is what gives that failure its meaning: because the concept definitions, probe protocol, intervention, controls, and decision rule were each fixed before the data that would judge them, the pipeline could reject the intended interpretation rather than absorb it. One valid activation-manipulation experiment exists in validity v5 — the tenuki-distance proxy — and no causal claim extends to the forcing proxy, the urgency proxy, or any historical label.
